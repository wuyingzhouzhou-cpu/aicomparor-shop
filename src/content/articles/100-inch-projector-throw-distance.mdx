A 100-inch 16:9 screen is **87.2 inches wide**. Measure from the screen surface to the front of the lens and divide by 87.2 inches to find the throw ratio your room needs. At 9.2 feet, that is about **1.27:1**. The diagonal printed on the box is the wrong number to use as the divisor.

<h2 id="worked-example">A real room with a 2.8-meter shelf distance</h2>

A Reddit shopper described a dark bedroom, a 100-inch screen about 2.8 meters away and two possible locations: a shelf over the bed or a ceiling mount near a light. The same geometry works in US units. The desired image is 2.21 meters wide, so a lens at 2.8 meters needs roughly 1.27:1. A fixed 1.2:1 model such as the BenQ GV31 would need about 2.66 meters (8 feet 9 inches) for that picture. The difference is around six inches, enough to change which shelf position works. <h2 id="shelf">Measure from the lens and check the vertical drawing</h2>

Mark the lens position on the shelf with tape. Allow for the body behind it, power and HDMI plugs, and the clearances in the manual. If the model lists a zoom range, check that your calculated ratio falls inside it. Then look at the installation diagram for a level projector. Offset determines where the image sits vertically relative to the lens. A high shelf can put the picture above the screen even when the throw distance is perfect. [How manufacturers describe throw ratio](https://www.benq.com/en-us/knowledge-center/knowledge/what-is-throw-ratio-for-projectors.html).

<h2 id="mount">Decide between moving the shelf and buying a mount</h2>

If the image is too high, first try a lower shelf or a model with suitable optical lens shift. Digital keystone can square up a tilted picture, but it cannot shorten the lens’s required throw. For ceiling placement, check that the model permits inverted installation and consult its ceiling diagram. Confirm the attachment pattern, rated load, power route and clearance from the light before ordering a mount.
