<h2 id="juice">Juicy lemon with a soft base</h2>

In [a discussion about scents that smell like lemon juice](https://www.reddit.com/r/Perfumes/comments/1xw9pu1/looking_for_a_perfume_that_smells_like_straight/), one shopper wanted lemon to stay the main note. A reply singled out C.O. Bigelow Lemon No. 1999 for its realistic, juicy lemon and also noticed musk underneath. [C.O. Bigelow’s note list](https://bigelowchemists.com/products/c-o-bigelow-lemon-eau-de-parfum-no-1999) includes lemon and white musk. Treat it as a sample lead if you like the idea of lemon settling into a softer base. If the musk is the part you want to avoid, compare it with a second sample before ordering a bottle.

<h2 id="peel">Bright peel and aromatic herbs</h2>

Several commenters brought up 4711 Original Eau de Cologne as an old-school citrus option. One described it as short-lived and suggested reapplying. Try it for a quick citrus lift if you also enjoy the herbal and neroli character of a traditional cologne. <h2 id="dessert">Creamy lemon dessert</h2>

The same thread offered Lattafa Victoria as a lemon meringue option. [Lattafa USA](https://www.lattafa-usa.com/products/victoria) lists lemon meringue pie, neroli, and vanilla. That makes it an informative sample alongside a classic cologne. Both open with a lemon idea, but the sweetness and the base determine which one you'll want to wear. If cake and custard notes tend to take over for you, start with the other two profiles.

<h2 id="test">A three-step sample test</h2>

1. Write down the smell you want: fresh juice, fragrant peel, or lemon curd.
2. Smell each sample at application and once it has settled. Note whether it turns musky, herbal, candy-like, or creamy.
3. Compare the delivered cost of the smallest sizes and check application format. A larger splash can work for frequent use, while a small spray is easier to carry.
